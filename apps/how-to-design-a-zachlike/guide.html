A guide to open-ended engineering puzzles

How to design a Zachlike

A Zachlike asks the player to build an executable artifact, prove it against an exact behavioural specification, then improve it along competing dimensions.

The goal is a small set of understandable rules whose interactions produce a large space of solutions. The player builds something, runs it, inspects what happened, and revises it.

A precise test separates correctness from quality. Passing ends the required puzzle. Speed, size, cost, robustness, elegance, and other qualities create voluntary second puzzles.

The first four chapters describe one conceptual dependency:

Core · Domain → Mechanics → Systems → Puzzles
Product · Presentation · Teaching · Optimization · Story
Practice · Prototype → Evidence → Revision ↺

Domain supplies the process, verbs, and natural constraints. Mechanics define the available operations. Systems are what those rules do together. Puzzles select and pressure the resulting possibility space.

Read these chapters in order because each gives the next a basis. The arrows describe how design decisions derive from one another, not a one-way production schedule. Prototype the chain as a loop: puzzle evidence can force changes to the system, the mechanic set, or even the domain.

Around the core

Four more concerns turn a sound puzzle system into a complete game:

  • Presentation makes rules, state, causality, failure, and cost inspectable and makes revision cheap.
  • Teaching turns the campaign into a curriculum without turning the tutorial into the puzzle.
  • Optimization gives a correct machine a second life through conflicting measures, comparison, and sharing.
  • Story gives the work a purpose and helps choose which artifacts and briefs belong.

These are not extra stages after the dependency chain. They surround it, reveal it, and must enter the prototype early enough to test the decisions they change. The final chapter closes the loop by turning the whole structure into a prototyping and playtesting practice.

What remains outside the scope

This guide does not prescribe engine architecture, art direction, audio production, platform strategy, budgets, publishing, or marketing. It states what the player-facing system must make possible, not how to implement it.

The separate design-element catalog is vocabulary, not a specification. A coherent game is defined as much by what it omits as by what it includes.

1 · Domain

The domain is the process the game simulates: the slice of reality, engineering, or invented physics the player learns to manipulate. It is not the setting or art style.

A useful domain creates a work fantasy: the player feels like the person who makes a particular kind of artifact behave. It supplies verbs, pressures, representations, and natural briefs. It narrows which mechanics belong without pretending that realism can make every design decision.

Start from a process

Choose something a person could perform, not a broad subject. Designing a cheap consumer device, as in SHENZHEN I/O, is more useful than “electronics” because it implies a product, components, signals, a board, a budget, and operating requirements.

A promising process has four properties:

  • It produces an artifact. The player can name what they are making, what it receives, and what it must do.
  • It supplies verbs. The player can route, join, split, schedule, store, transform, or test something.
  • Its relevant state fits in one view. The player can inspect the causes that matter.
  • It contains pressure. Capacity, timing, reach, scarcity, or irreversibility can force decisions.

Four shipped Zachlikes show the pattern:

Game and process Verbs Pressure derived from the process
SpaceChem · molecular production route, grab, bond, unbond, synchronize two waldos share a small reactor and must coordinate the molecules they hold
Infinifactory · assembly-line engineering route, push, rotate, weld, destroy products must align in three-dimensional space while moving
SHENZHEN I/O · product electronics wire, program, sense, signal components, pins, power, and code are scarce
EXAPUNKS · network intrusion move, copy, read, write, message programs travel through a constrained host topology

These games simplify their domains aggressively, but each simplification protects the work fantasy and the decisions it needs. Observe or research a process before depending on its metaphor.

Test its yield and cost

Ask six questions before committing:

Question Evidence to seek
What is the process? You can name it briefly and list its objects and transformations
What does the player make? The artifact has inputs, outputs, operating conditions, and a reason to exist
What does it generate? Several mechanics and constraints arise without arbitrary exceptions
What can the player see? Important state, paths, and limits have concrete representations
What intuition transfers? The metaphor helps the player predict a rule before running it
What must be taught? Every required fact can be supplied clearly inside the game

Specialist vocabulary is a real onboarding cost. Invented physics avoids assumed expertise, but it still needs consistent rules and readable representations.

Depart from realism wherever accuracy would hide state, add labour, or create an exception the player cannot predict. Keep the fiction coherent enough that the simplification feels deliberate.

The domain is ready when you can name the process and its artifacts, derive several verbs and pressures, teach all required knowledge, and use the metaphor to reject incoherent rules.

Next: §2 turns those verbs into a small rule set.

2 · Mechanics

A mechanic is a rule, not a themed object. A conveyor, wire, pipe, and queue may be the same mechanic when they create the same decisions.

Count a second mechanic when its rule changes what players decide or predict, how they fail, or what they can optimize.

Start with as few rules as the system needs. A new exception is not depth, and documentation growing faster than possibility is a warning.

Build a construction language

Design a language for making systems, not a bag of themed parts. Define three things before expanding the palette:

Layer Question Examples
State What exists, persists, and can change? SpaceChem's atoms and bonds; Infinifactory's blocks and orientation; SHENZHEN I/O's signals and registers
Operations Which small actions transform that state? Opus Magnum's grab, rotate, bond, and transmute; EXAPUNKS' move, copy, read, and write
Coordination How do operations interact across time and space? TIS-100's lockstep nodes, SpaceChem's waldo paths, EXAPUNKS' host topology

A finished solution usually has an architecture — where work happens — and a schedule — when it happens. Geometry may embody either one, but the rules must make both predictable.

Ask what new mental model this language makes the player learn. A conventional programming or construction model with new nouns may still be useful, but it gives up one of the form's strongest pleasures: discovering a genuinely different way to organize work. Originality is a prompt, not an excuse for obscurity; the language still has to survive the tests below.

Test every mechanic

Ask whether a mechanic deepens the whole system rather than whether it sounds interesting alone.

Criterion Question
Domain fit Does it feel like a consequence of the simulated process?
Interaction density Which other rules does it change in a strategically meaningful way?
Tension What must the player give up to use it?
Local predictability Can an experienced player predict its next effect?
Expressiveness Does it enable new architectures rather than more arrangements?

Cut a mechanic when one use dominates, it touches only one narrow subsystem, or it duplicates another rule without changing decisions.

Also cut it when explanation exceeds possibility or it needs an incoherent exception to the core system.

Choose a central pressure

Ask what the player is short of. One or two scarce things can organize an otherwise loose collection of rules.

Pressure The scarce thing Solving means
Space room, layers, instruction memory compressing
Time phases, collision windows, latency scheduling
Language instructions, registers, states expressing more with less
Flow capacity, ports, bandwidth routing and balancing
Certainty known cases, reliable components building for robustness
Stability rules that may alter other rules reasoning about semantics

If the honest answer is “nothing in particular,” check whether construction has become work without a decision.

Keep four related terms distinct:

Term Scope Function
Pressure the whole construction system A recurring scarcity that organizes decisions across puzzles
Constraint a rule or one puzzle A hard boundary on what the player may build or what counts as valid
Bottleneck one puzzle The facet of the central pressure that dominates that specification
Metric valid solutions A measure that compares quality after correctness

A cramped board can therefore be a constraint, packing its bottleneck, space the game's pressure, and footprint one metric. The terms describe different jobs even when they concern the same scarce thing.

The separate design-element catalog maps nine broad families. It is deliberately wider than the mechanic set: one mechanic as counted here — one rule the player learns as a unit — may span several construction-rule rows, while other rows name systemic phenomena or puzzle and product devices. Open it after you can name the domain and pressure; use it to find interactions and consequences, not to fill a palette.

The mechanic set is coherent when it fits on one hand, survives the five questions, and creates a pressure you can name in one sentence.

Next: §3 asks what those rules do together.

3 · Systems

A system is what emerges when mechanics interact. In TIS-100, nodes execute tiny programs in parallel, communicate only with their four neighbours, and block until a matching read and write meet. Those local rules produce behaviour none of them names: routing protocols, pipelines, starvation, and deadlocks whose timing depends on the whole network.

You write local rules; the system is what they do together. Useful complexity comes from composition — many small, unambiguous rules interacting — not from ambiguity inside one part. The machine must also be repeatable: once built, it can run again under the same conditions and produce the same visible sequence.

Freeze the system contract

Define and test the rules that every later mechanic depends on. Each category is a set of questions the system must always answer the same way:

  • Object identity and transformation. In SpaceChem, which atom does a waldo hold, and exactly when does a bond form or break?
  • Connections, capacity, and transfer. In TIS-100, which ports may communicate, and when does a read or write wait?
  • Time, update phases, and arbitration. In Opus Magnum, when do simultaneous arms act, and which collisions invalidate the cycle?
  • Failure, completion, and editing. In Infinifactory, what counts as a malformed product or a finished run, and what state resets before the next test?

These rules form the system contract because determinism rests on them: the same machine, input, rules, and seed must produce the same visible sequence. Seeded variation meets that standard when replaying a seed reproduces every failure exactly. A mature machine may loop forever or stop at a quota, but repetition must not change its semantics.

Revise unfinished rules freely. Once players save solutions against a released rule, change it only deliberately, visibly, and with compatibility in mind.

Design interactions, not inventories

For every pair of core mechanics, ask whether one changes the value, cost, timing, or failure modes of the other. Formal connectivity alone is not an interaction: an arm can load a belt, but if the belt treats every item alike, arm × belt has created no new decision.

Put the pairs in a matrix — mechanics on both axes, one cell per unordered pair. For n mechanics that is n(n−1)/2 cells; four mechanics produce six, seven produce twenty-one. Answer three questions per cell:

Pair New decision Visible consequence Dependency
A × B What becomes possible, costly, or preferable only together? How can the player predict and inspect it? Does either rule earn its place only through this pair?
TIS-100 port rendezvous × fixed topology A short route may block a node needed by another stream The active port and waiting instruction expose the stall No — each also interacts with program length and local memory

Do this. Fill the matrix, then read it two ways. A weak row — a mechanic that changes little no matter what it is paired with — is a cut candidate. A dominant cell — one pair carrying most of the system's interest — needs puzzle tests to show it creates more than one recipe.

Specify observable state

Define which facts any presentation must expose. Objects, values, and control need traceable paths; transformations need inspectable inputs, conditions, and outputs. The player must be able to see both the architecture and the schedule, even when timing is encoded in distance or layout.

Also define the earliest meaningful divergence the simulator can report. A collision, wrong value, missed deadline, overflow, or illegal state should preserve the failing moment and the causal state around it. §5 turns that requirement into debugging tools.

Turn important limits into visible properties:

Constraint Representation
capacity slots or lanes
reach geometry
time a tick, phase, or trace
collision occupied space
cost an explicit counter tied to the action that spends it

Local rules can create global complexity while staying locally predictable. Seven mechanisms do most of that work:

Mechanism Local rule Global consequence
Shared capacity a SHENZHEN I/O microcontroller serves several signals servicing one input delays another
Synchronization SpaceChem's two waldos act on the same cycle paths must agree about timing and collision
Feedback a TIS-100 value returns to an earlier node local state becomes a repeating control loop
Alternate routes a TIS-100 value can cross different nodes congestion moves to another part of the grid
Irreversibility an Opus Magnum glyph changes an atom operation order becomes a commitment
Limited state a TIS-100 node has ACC and BAK history must be distributed or encoded in timing
Topology EXAPUNKS behaviour follows host links changing routes changes coordination without new instructions

Backpressure appears directly in TIS-100: a node writing to a port waits until its neighbour reads, which can leave earlier nodes waiting behind it. A cycle of such waits is a deadlock. If the system permits either, show where the chain or cycle begins.

Hidden state, inconsistent exceptions, visual ambiguity, and sheer scale usually add confusion rather than useful depth.

The system is coherent when its core rules are explicit, several interactions create decisions, and an experienced player can predict a tie, collision, and full container.

Next: §4 selects those interactions with puzzle specifications.

4 · Puzzles

A puzzle combines a specification, a board, and a palette. Together they state what must happen, where the player may build, and which parts are available.

Author the specification first

Decide what observable behaviour passes before exploring layouts. The engine checks output against that puzzle specification, not resemblance to a reference build.

Write the specification in one sentence:

Given [inputs and conditions], produce [outputs and behaviour], while never [invalid state].

Then specify the legal input range, required outputs, quota or duration, available workspace and tools, hard limits, failure conditions, and exact pass condition. The specification does not prescribe construction steps or depend on rules outside the published system contract.

Build a small test set:

  • a simple case that proves basic understanding;
  • a boundary case;
  • a case that breaks the most obvious shortcut;
  • a case that exercises the puzzle's central pressure; and
  • enough variation to require a general design.

Unseen cases may vary values, but they must not introduce new semantics. When a run fails, report the smallest useful counterexample.

The verifier shapes the work. A repeated quota rewards throughput; an input-output stream rewards generalization; a test suite rewards branching and state; a final-state target rewards construction and emergence.

Keep internal reference builds to prove solvability and expose accidental restrictions. They are evidence about the specification, never the definition of the answer. Authoring from a specification does not remove the duty to verify that somebody can solve it.

Choose the floor and the ceiling

Every puzzle has two difficulty targets:

  • Solution floor: the effort needed to make anything correct.
  • Optimization ceiling: the depth available after it works.

Let an obvious, clumsy solution pass. Do not require an optimized score to continue unless satisfying that bound is the puzzle itself. Approachability comes from lowering the floor without flattening the ceiling.

Pressure one facet

Name the bottleneck before building the puzzle: routing, synchronization, storage, geometry, state, collision, transformation order, or topology. The bottleneck selects one facet of the central pressure defined in §2; a game whose scarce thing is flow pressures routing and storage before geometry. Board and palette constraints then determine how directly the player must confront it.

Board Effect Where difficulty lives
Cramped Few layouts fit decomposition and packing
Open Honest solutions fit optimization and self-imposed goals
Graph Nodes constrain connection communication topology and protocol

Use the palette as a balance lever. Too loose trivializes the bottleneck; too tight funnels the player toward one architecture.

Separate reasoning from labour. Raise a quota only until startup, steady state, or a timing interaction becomes visible. If the insight arrives quickly and the remaining work is repeated placement, retiming, or waiting, shrink or reframe the puzzle.

Do this. Change the bottleneck before changing the numbers. Difficulty should come from a new decision, not a larger quota or smaller board by default.

Distinguish novelty from openness

Puzzle distinctiveness compares puzzles across a campaign: can the player reuse the same process with trivial edits? Solution diversity compares answers to one puzzle: can valid builds differ in decomposition, topology, scheduling, use of state, or metric priority?

They are separate tests. A campaign can contain distinct puzzles that each funnel toward one answer, or an intentionally reinforcing puzzle that revisits a known process but remains rich to optimize. Label reinforcement honestly; do not present repetition as a new idea.

For each open puzzle, construct two plausible same-palette references from different solution families. Two references show possibility, not prevalence, but they expose a specification that accidentally permits only one recipe. Scores alone do not show diversity: unrelated machines can share a score, and one architecture can span many scores.

Use antagonistic metrics

A metric belongs when it changes which architectures are attractive. Two or three measures are usually enough. Speed, space, resources, program size, power, and robustness work only when improving one can worsen another.

The solutions where no measure can improve without another getting worse form a Pareto frontier. Aim for a populated frontier whose specialists look structurally different. If one solution wins every metric, change the cost model, palette, or specification. Show the measures separately; a weighted total hides the trade-off.

A hard limit defines validity. A metric compares valid solutions. Leave some valued qualities — symmetry, readability, choreography, audacity — unscored so the graphs do not exhaust taste.

A puzzle is structurally sound when its specification is explicit, its tests are fair, its bottleneck is named, a crude build proves the floor, and an open puzzle admits a plausible contrasting family with room above it.

Next: §5 makes the system editable, inspectable, and debuggable.

5 · Presentation and debugging

Presentation is visual design, sound, animation, interface, editing, feedback, documentation, and terminology. It cannot create mechanical depth. It determines whether the player can perceive, predict, and work with the depth that exists.

A decorative skin is presentation. A visible port, capacity slot, timing trace, or collision boundary becomes part of play because it changes what the player can reason about.

Do not postpone this as polish. An illegible prototype cannot tell you whether a playtest exposed a system problem or merely hid a sound rule.

Make causality visible

At rest and while running, the game should answer six questions:

Question Required evidence
What does this part do? Shape, ports, labels, and animation share a stable visual grammar
Where can things go? Direction, connection, crossing, reach, and capacity are visible before execution
What is happening now? Active, waiting, blocked, full, empty, and faulted appear on or beside the part
What caused this state? Material, data, and control can be traced from source to consequence
Why did the run fail? The earliest divergence is preserved and expected state is aligned with actual state
What made this score? Expensive operations, idle capacity, long paths, and waste are tied to the metric they change

Preserve object identity through movement and transformation. If an item disappears into a process and an unrelated result appears elsewhere, the player cannot connect architecture to behaviour.

TIS-100 prints values on ports and highlights the instruction each node is executing, so a blocked transfer can be traced across the grid. Opus Magnum makes an arm's program, swept space, held atom, and collision visible in the same view. Both games remain difficult without making their current state secret.

Use colour, sound, and motion redundantly. Required information must survive grayscale, silence, pausing, and reduced motion. At overview scale preserve identity, direction, state, and faults; reveal detail as the player zooms in.

Make revision cheap

Open-ended construction is repeated refactoring. The time between an idea and its first observable consequence should stay short.

  • Make place, delete, rotate, connect, and edit-instruction actions immediate.
  • Support region selection, move, copy, paste, rotate or mirror, and deep undo and redo.
  • Preserve camera, selection, partial edits, and simulation state when switching between the workspace, specification, documentation, tests, and scores.
  • Show reach, collision, occupancy, and illegal connections before the player commits an edit.
  • Allow several named solution variants so experimentation never requires destroying the known-good build.

Let partial and incorrect machines run whenever they can produce meaningful behaviour. Missing output is an unmet specification, not a reason to disable the simulator.

Treat the simulator as the main interface

Keep run, pause, reset, single-step, and useful speeds reachable at all times. One step advances one defined unit of simulated time. Show the current cycle, case, and phase.

When an instruction or controller acts, highlight both it and the affected object. Waiting and blocking are states, not generic errors: show what a part needs, what holds the resource, and whether the machine is merely slow or deadlocked.

Stop automatically at the first meaningful failure, preserve that state, and provide a readable trace around it. A cascade of later faults must not bury the first place the player's model diverged from the simulator.

Failure can be pleasurable to watch, but it must remain fast, local, informative, and costless to retry.

Be a trustworthy development environment

Use one term for every part, state, port, and operation across the editor, specification, manual, tutorial, and error messages. Pair unfamiliar icons with words until mastery is plausible.

The same machine, input, rules, and seed must replay identically. Ordering, limits, tests, and scoring cannot contain hidden preferences or unexplained exceptions. A strict simulator feels fair when it exposes the rule, the relevant state, and the exact reason a run stopped.

Presentation works when a new observer can inspect a paused machine and describe its broad strategy, a failed run points to the first real fault, and changing an idea costs less effort than rebuilding it.

Next: §6 arranges these rules and tools into a curriculum.

6 · Teaching and campaign

The player is learning one system. Puzzles are the vehicle; progress is fluency — predicting behaviour before running, recognizing reusable structures, diagnosing failures, and choosing an architecture on purpose.

A campaign is therefore a curriculum whether or not it was designed as one.

Teach purpose before syntax

The first lesson is the job: given these inputs, produce these outputs. A placement walkthrough can teach gestures while leaving the player unable to explain what the machine is for.

Start in the real editor with the real rules. Pre-place enough context that the minimum correct solution needs one meaningful action, then remove the scaffolding. If the first solution requires five unfamiliar actions at once, reduce the minimum solution instead of adding more explanation.

SpaceChem exposed the failure mode: its first complete loop required the player to understand input, grab, path arrows, drop, and output together. Later Zachlikes lowered the floor by making the first useful artifact require fewer simultaneous concepts.

For each mechanic:

  1. show its purpose;
  2. demonstrate the smallest useful action;
  3. let the player reproduce it;
  4. ask for a simple independent use;
  5. reinforce it in a changed context; and
  6. combine it with earlier mechanics later.

Use counterexamples to correct likely false rules. Prefer a clearer representation or simpler rule over another paragraph of instructions.

Alternate novelty and reinforcement

Give every puzzle an explicit curriculum role:

Role Work it does
Introduce One new idea is the only unknown
Reinforce A known process becomes fluent and leaves room to optimize
Vary Familiar rules appear under a different board, input, or pressure
Combine Two established ideas interact for the first time
Capstone The player chooses and coordinates several ideas independently

Constant novelty turns the campaign into a series of exams. Constant repetition turns it into labour. Alternate the two deliberately, and do not call a reinforcement puzzle a new idea.

Kaizen: A Factory Story deliberately used easier reinforcement puzzles so players could apply a process they had just learned and then explore its optimization surface. That is different from padding a campaign with a larger quota.

Teach collision order, simultaneous action, blocking, overflow, capacity, and test timing before they can cause an expensive failure. Players may discover strategies; they should not discover undocumented semantics.

Curate the order after playtesting

Author more specifications than you need, then watch them played. Remove accidental logical duplicates, fill genuine conceptual gaps, and reorder the remainder. Difficulty order is an evidence problem, not an intuition problem.

When a puzzle becomes a wall, first ask whether it introduced several burdens at once, depended on an edge case that was never demonstrated, or simply required too much construction after the insight.

For a broad campaign, open small batches and require only some puzzles to advance. Put the hardest material after the narrative ending or mark it clearly optional. One mandatory puzzle should not end both play and story for an otherwise capable player.

Use documentation as reference and fiction

Manuals, datasheets, zines, diagrams, and reference cards can make rule lookup part of the work fantasy. They should be searchable or well indexed, use the same terminology as the interface, remain available without losing work, and state edge cases explicitly.

Documentation supports a good first-use path; it does not repair an unreadable mechanic. §8 covers how the same artifact can also carry voice and world-building.

Teaching works when the player understands the job before following syntax, each new burden arrives in isolation, established ideas receive time for reinforcement, punishing semantics were demonstrated harmlessly first, and a stuck player has another route forward.

Next: §7 gives a solved puzzle its second life.

7 · Optimization and sharing

Correctness and quality are separate games. §4 defines the conflicting measures that reshape a solution space. This chapter begins after the first valid machine exists.

Optimization should feel voluntary. The game does not need to withhold progress, currency, or story to make improvement meaningful; the machine, the cost model, and visible evidence of better approaches can supply the challenge.

Reveal the distribution after success

Show per-puzzle histograms only after the first correct solution. The player first owns an answer; then the distribution asks a new question.

A global leaderboard makes nearly everyone a distant loser and rewards cheating at the extreme. A histogram makes the middle legible. It shows how far an improvement might be, exposes clusters that may represent different solution families, and lets an ordinary player choose a reachable target.

SpaceChem established the pattern with separate histograms for cycles, symbols, and reactors. The graphs include first-pass and optimized solutions together, so beating the middle remains a plausible personal challenge rather than a bid for a world record.

Show each antagonistic metric separately, with the current result and personal best marked. Do not collapse the measures into one total or reveal another player's architecture without an explicit spoiler action.

Make cost causal

Connect every score to behaviour the player can inspect. Show which operation spends power, what adds latency, where footprint grows, which component creates waste, and where capacity sits idle.

An opaque total supports trial and error. A causal cost model supports engineering.

Leave some qualities unscored. Symmetry, readability, robustness beyond the tests, elegant motion, and audacious excess give communities room to develop tastes the official graphs do not define.

Protect experiments

Never make improvement require overwriting the machine that worked. Support named solutions, duplication and branching, a known-good baseline, metric-specific variants, thumbnails, and recorded scores.

Comparison should make specialists visible. When the fastest, cheapest, smallest, and most legible solutions are different artifacts, the player has several meaningful reasons to return.

Design the export with the system

A shared number says less than a shared machine. The export may be a looping animation, short video, schematic, code listing, or importable solution, but it must reveal the intelligence of the answer rather than only its final effect.

Opus Magnum's stable two-dimensional view, repeating machines, and visible arm choreography make its GIFs readable without editing a camera. EXAPUNKS exposes the opposite risk: agent movement alone reveals less when the important reasoning lives in code off-screen.

Include the puzzle identity, relevant execution, metrics, and enough program or structure to understand the trick. Keep the frame stable, make the result easy to produce in one action, and mark spoilers. If importing and modifying shared work suits the community, preserve authorship and make remixing explicit.

The export format feeds back into the mechanic and presentation. A beautiful motion that hides its control logic is a weak explanation; a complete code dump that hides the machine's choreography is equally incomplete.

The second game works when every score has an inspectable cause, the distributions appear only after ownership, experiments preserve the working baseline, and a shared artifact makes the clever part visible.

Next: §8 gives the work, the metrics, and the campaign a reason to exist.

8 · Story and work

Story gives the puzzles something to be about. It helps select artifacts, constraints, clients, failures, and reasons to optimize. It cannot be pasted on after those decisions and still do that work.

Start with the core activity and the core story, then grow them together. A puzzle may begin with a mechanical need or a story beat, but it earns its place only when both sides agree.

Let the world choose the briefs

The domain supplies possible products; the story chooses which ones matter now. A client, incident, research question, production order, or institutional demand turns an abstract specification into a job.

Ask of every brief:

  • Why does this artifact exist?
  • Who needs it and under which conditions?
  • Which functions or constraints follow from that situation?
  • What does completing it reveal about the world or relationship?

If the setting can be replaced without changing the artifact, functions, constraints, or documents, it is wallpaper. Revise the brief or admit that the fiction is decorative.

In SHENZHEN I/O, consumer products such as signs, controllers, and novelty devices generate their own ports, signals, budgets, and test conditions. The briefs also reveal who commissioned the work, so the same specification carries character and system design.

Put story in the interface

Use media that belong to the work: emails, requests, manuals, logs, specifications, invoices, zines, test reports, and the products themselves. The same artifact can carry rules, voice, and world-building when each role remains clear.

TIS-100's printable computer manual presents the instruction set and the mystery of the machine as one object. EXAPUNKS' Trash World News does the same with a hacker zine. Neither format excuses hidden semantics; both make necessary reference material feel native to the role.

Keep the mandatory channel short and place it at the edge of the build-run-inspect-revise loop. Put deeper material in an optional, re-readable tier. Do not trigger irreplaceable dialogue during execution or make the player leave the workspace to recover a missed requirement.

Requirements must remain extractable from voice. Flavour may complicate motive; it must not hide inputs, outputs, limits, or failure conditions.

Give optimization a meaning

Metrics can express production economics, scarce material, limited power, urgency, secrecy, professional pride, or the standards of a demanding institution. The fiction need not reward every improvement, but it should make the measured qualities feel native to the work.

Likewise, a shareable artifact should look like something this world or profession might value: a demonstration, report, schematic, listing, recording, or proof of craft.

Use tone to widen the door honestly

A realistic-looking discipline may trigger assumed expertise before the player meets the actual rules. Story and art can make the role inviting, but they must not promise a different activity from the one the system delivers. Translate specialist knowledge into learnable rules; do not disguise unexplained complexity as authenticity.

Story is integrated when it helps choose the work, each brief changes because of who requested it, required information stays clear, deeper fiction remains optional and re-readable, and the reasons to build and improve belong to the same world.

Next: §9 turns the whole guide into a working method.

9 · Prototype, test, and revise

Do not begin with a full campaign. Build the smallest version that can prove the system, the editor, the curriculum, and the post-solve loop belong together.

Make a six-puzzle vertical slice

Puzzle Purpose
Orientation One input, one output, one primitive, and the real editor
Second verb A new operation in isolation
Reinforcement Familiar actions in a changed context
Coordination Two established operations interact
Divergence The first specification that reliably produces distinct architectures
Capstone A forgiving first pass with strong optimization tension

Build the actual runner and debugging controls, a representative test harness, at least two conflicting score axes, provisional histograms, named solution variants, and one real export format. These features change how puzzles are authored; postponing them means testing a different game.

Write the one-page definition

Before expanding the campaign, record the decisions every later puzzle depends on:

Field Decision to record
Experience promise What the player should feel by repeatedly doing which work
Domain and artifact The process, products, verbs, and natural constraints
Construction language State, operations, and coordination model
System contract Identity, transfer, time, arbitration, failure, completion, editing
Central pressure What the player is short of and why
Metrics Which axes conflict and how their specialists differ
Curriculum What is introduced, reinforced, varied, combined, and optional
Story channel Which in-world medium carries briefs, reference, and voice
Audience floor How crude a correct solution the intended audience can reach
Non-goals What the project will deliberately omit

Treat this as a decision record, not a prophecy. Change it when prototypes or players disprove it, then revisit every dependent puzzle.

Use three evidence streams

Evidence What it reveals
Fresh-player sessions Goal comprehension, control discovery, mental models, tutorial walls, avoidable complexity
Long-form expert testing Broken tests, duplicate puzzles, dominant strategies, weak metrics, late spikes, ceiling depth
Live telemetry Where problems cluster across many players and whether post-solve systems change behaviour

In fresh sessions, give only the information the shipped game provides and do not coach. Watch hesitation, false assumptions, ignored controls, and the first point where a player cannot explain what happened.

For telemetry, record only what you can use and collect it with consent and privacy safeguards. Useful events include puzzle starts and completions, time to first run and first correct run, resets, exits, returns after solving, score improvements after seeing a distribution, exports, and a short post-solve difficulty rating.

Metrics show where a problem is. Observation and interviews explain why.

Diagnose the layer that failed

Symptom Likely cause First response
Every player rebuilds the same machine Specification or palette is prescriptive Widen the specification and test another solution family
Players guess at failures State or causality is hidden Preserve the first divergence and expose its dependencies
Tutorials require constant explanation Minimum solution or representation is too complex Reduce the first task before adding prose
Every puzzle feels like a new exam No reinforcement Reuse known ideas under a changed context
One architecture wins every score Metrics or costs move together Create pairwise trade-offs and retest the frontier
One puzzle ends the campaign Several burdens or too much labour arrived together Split, bridge, branch, or move it after the ending
The story could be swapped without effect Fiction is wallpaper Derive the artifact and constraints from the world
Shared results hide the clever part Export omits program, structure, or timing Redesign the view or include an importable artifact

Use release gates

Before authoring more content, ask:

  • System: Can a few primitives express surprising behaviour without special-case parts?
  • Specification: Can the player state exactly why a failed run failed?
  • Diversity: Do several correct solutions differ structurally?
  • Floor: Can the intended audience make a crude correct artifact?
  • Ceiling: Can experts keep finding meaningful improvements?
  • Trade-off: Does improving one score sometimes worsen another?
  • Curriculum: Are purpose, reinforcement, combination, and optional mastery ordered cleanly?
  • World: Does each brief reveal something about the setting or work?
  • Artifact: Does the share format reveal what is clever?
  • Evidence: Have fresh players, experts, and observed behaviour all changed the design?

If several gates fail, do not write more levels. Change the system or the product layer that is hiding it.

The working rule. Build the smallest game that delivers the complete loop, then improve it by watching people use it. A puzzle sheet can prove an intention; only a running machine and another person's reasoning can test it.
