A guide to abstract puzzle mechanics

How to design a Zachlike

A Zachlike asks the player to build a machine, run it, inspect what happened, and revise it. This guide is about designing the puzzle system at the heart of that loop.

The goal is a small set of understandable rules whose interactions produce a large space of solutions.

This guide follows one dependency chain:

Domain → Mechanics → Systems → Puzzles

Domain supplies the process, verbs, and natural constraints. Mechanics define the available operations. Systems are what those rules do together. Puzzles select and pressure the resulting possibility space.

Read the four chapters in order. Each narrows the decisions available to the next.

Presentation is the lens

Presentation is not a fifth layer. It cannot create mechanical depth; it reveals or hides that depth.

This guide assumes the player can inspect rules, state, causality, and failure. It does not cover how to achieve that through interface design, art, sound, animation, editing tools, or accessibility work.

Outside the scope

This guide does not cover:

  • teaching or campaign progression;
  • story, setting, or character framing;
  • implementation and editor architecture;
  • production planning or playtesting practice; or
  • community features, rankings, and score distributions.

Metrics appear only when they change the abstract solution space of a puzzle. Optimization culture and post-solve metagames do not.

The separate mechanics catalog is vocabulary, not a specification. A coherent game is defined as much by what it omits as by what it includes.

1 · Domain

The domain is the process the game simulates: the slice of reality, engineering, or invented physics the player learns to manipulate. It is not the setting or art style.

A useful domain supplies verbs, pressures, and representations. It narrows which mechanics belong without pretending that realism can make every design decision.

Start from a process

Choose something a person could perform, not a broad subject. Voicing a pipe organ is more useful than “music” because it implies actions, equipment, shared wind, and a bounded workspace.

A promising process has three properties:

  • It supplies verbs. The player can route, join, split, schedule, store, transform, or test something.
  • Its relevant state fits in one view. The player can inspect the causes that matter.
  • It contains pressure. Capacity, timing, reach, scarcity, or irreversibility can force decisions.

Four contrasting prompts show the pattern:

Process Verbs Candidate pressure
Railway interlocking set, lock, clear, release Incompatible routes cannot claim one block
Organ building rank, wind, couple, voice One wind supply serves everything sounding
Darkroom developing expose, develop, stop, fix Baths are timed and some changes are irreversible
Ore smelting line feed, move, smelt, cast One furnace's rate paces every line that feeds it

These are prompts, not endorsements. Observe or research the process before depending on its metaphor.

Test its yield and cost

Ask five questions before committing:

Question Evidence to seek
What is the process? You can name it briefly and list its objects and transformations
What does it generate? Several mechanics and constraints arise without arbitrary exceptions
What can the player see? Important state, paths, and limits have concrete representations
What intuition transfers? The metaphor helps the player predict a rule before running it
What must be taught? Every required fact can be supplied clearly inside the game

Specialist vocabulary is a real onboarding cost. Invented physics avoids assumed expertise, but it still needs consistent rules and readable representations.

Depart from realism wherever accuracy would hide state, add labour, or create an exception the player cannot predict. Keep the fiction coherent enough that the simplification feels deliberate.

The domain is ready when you can name the process, derive several verbs and pressures, teach all required knowledge, and use the metaphor to reject incoherent rules.

Next: §2 turns those verbs into a small rule set.

2 · Mechanics

A mechanic is a rule, not a themed object. A conveyor, wire, pipe, and queue may be the same mechanic when they create the same decisions.

Count a second mechanic when its rule changes what players decide or predict, how they fail, or what they can optimize.

Start with as few rules as the system needs. A new exception is not depth, and documentation growing faster than possibility is a warning.

Test every mechanic

Ask whether a mechanic deepens the whole system rather than whether it sounds interesting alone.

Criterion Question
Domain fit Does it feel like a consequence of the simulated process?
Interaction density Which other rules does it change in a strategically meaningful way?
Tension What must the player give up to use it?
Local predictability Can an experienced player predict its next effect?
Expressiveness Does it enable new architectures rather than more arrangements?

Cut a mechanic when one use dominates, it touches only one narrow subsystem, or it duplicates another rule without changing decisions.

Also cut it when explanation exceeds possibility or it needs an incoherent exception to the core system.

Choose a central pressure

Ask what the player is short of. One or two scarce things can organize an otherwise loose collection of rules.

Pressure The scarce thing Solving means
Space room, layers, instruction memory compressing
Time phases, collision windows, latency scheduling
Language instructions, registers, states expressing more with less
Flow capacity, ports, bandwidth routing and balancing
Certainty known cases, reliable components building for robustness
Stability rules that may alter other rules reasoning about semantics

If the honest answer is “nothing in particular,” check whether construction has become work without a decision.

The separate mechanics catalog maps nine broad families. Its rows are finer-grained than the count above: one mechanic as counted here — one rule the player learns as a unit — often spans several rows. Open it after you can name the domain and pressure; use it to find interactions, not to fill a palette.

The mechanic set is coherent when it fits on one hand, survives the five questions, and creates a pressure you can name in one sentence.

Next: §3 asks what those rules do together.

3 · Systems

A system is what emerges when mechanics interact. Take the smelting line from §1. Three local rules — a belt moves items one cell per tick, an arm moves one item between adjacent cells, a furnace consumes two items to emit one — already produce behaviour none of them mentions: queues, stalls, races over a shared cell, throughput that depends on layout.

You write local rules; the system is what they do together. Useful complexity comes from composition — many small, unambiguous rules interacting — not from ambiguity inside one part.

Freeze the core contract

Define and test the rules that every later mechanic depends on. Each category is a set of questions the system must always answer the same way:

  • Object identity and transformation. When are two items the same item? What exactly does the furnace emit for each input it accepts?
  • Connections, capacity, and transfer. What may connect to what, how much fits in one cell or container, and when does a transfer refuse to happen?
  • Time, update phases, and arbitration. How long is a tick, in what order do parts act inside it, and when two arms grab the same item in the same tick, which wins?
  • Failure, completion, and editing. What counts as a crash, a spill, or a finished run, and what may the player change while the machine runs?

These rules are the contract because determinism rests on them: the same machine, input, rules, and seed must produce the same visible sequence. Seeded variation meets that standard when replaying a seed reproduces every failure exactly.

Revise unfinished rules freely. Once players save solutions against a released rule, change it only deliberately, visibly, and with compatibility in mind.

Design interactions, not inventories

For every pair of core mechanics, ask whether one changes the value, cost, timing, or failure modes of the other. Formal connectivity alone is not an interaction: an arm can load a belt, but if the belt treats every item alike, arm × belt has created no new decision.

Put the pairs in a matrix — mechanics on both axes, one cell per unordered pair. For n mechanics that is n(n−1)/2 cells; four mechanics produce six, seven produce twenty-one. Answer three questions per cell:

Pair New decision Visible consequence Dependency
A × B What becomes possible, costly, or preferable only together? How can the player predict and inspect it? Does either rule earn its place only through this pair?
belt × furnace Feed rate becomes a choice: an overfed furnace backs its belt up The queue is visible on the belt itself No — each also interacts with the arm

Do this. Fill the matrix, then read it two ways. A weak row — a mechanic that changes little no matter what it is paired with — is a cut candidate. A dominant cell — one pair carrying most of the system's interest — needs puzzle tests to show it creates more than one recipe.

Specify observable state

Define which facts any presentation must expose. Objects, values, and control need traceable paths; transformations need inspectable inputs, conditions, and outputs.

Turn important limits into visible properties:

Constraint Representation
capacity slots or lanes
reach geometry
time a tick, phase, or trace
collision occupied space
cost an explicit counter tied to the action that spends it

Local rules can create global complexity while staying locally predictable. Seven mechanisms do most of that work:

Mechanism Local rule Global consequence
Shared capacity one furnace serves two lines feeding one line starves the other
Synchronization parts act on fixed phases distant parts must agree about timing
Feedback output routed back as input small changes amplify or damp instead of staying local
Alternate routes more than one path reaches the goal congestion moves rather than disappears
Irreversibility the furnace cannot un-smelt operation order becomes a commitment
Limited state a latch holds one bit history must be encoded in position or timing
Topology behaviour follows the wiring rearranging connections changes outcomes without new parts

Backpressure is a full destination stalling supply upstream: the furnace stops accepting, the belt behind it fills, the arm behind that waits. Deadlock is a cycle of parts each waiting on the next. If the system permits either, show where the chain or cycle begins.

Hidden state, inconsistent exceptions, visual ambiguity, and sheer scale usually add confusion rather than useful depth.

The system is coherent when its core rules are explicit, several interactions create decisions, and an experienced player can predict a tie, collision, and full container.

4 · Puzzles

A puzzle combines a specification, a board, and a palette. Together they state what must happen, where the player may build, and which parts are available.

Author the contract first

Decide what observable behaviour passes before exploring layouts. The engine checks output against that contract, not resemblance to a reference build.

The specification states inputs, required outputs, quota, hard limits, and failure conditions. It does not prescribe construction steps or depend on rules outside the published system.

Keep internal reference builds to prove solvability and expose accidental restrictions. They are evidence about the contract, never the definition of the answer.

Let an obvious solution pass and score poorly. Optimization becomes meaningful when a player can first satisfy the contract, then discover a different decomposition, topology, or schedule.

Do this. Write the contract, build the obvious route, then find a contrasting route with the same palette. If the alternative fails only because it differs from your favourite, fix the validator.

Pressure one facet

Name the bottleneck before building the puzzle: routing, synchronization, storage, geometry, state, collision, transformation order, or topology. Pick the facet that expresses the central pressure from §2; a game whose scarce thing is flow pressures routing and storage before geometry.

If the honest answer is “the previous puzzle, but more,” re-aim it or cut it. Raise a quota only until startup, steady state, or a timing interaction becomes visible.

Board Effect Where difficulty lives
Cramped Few layouts fit decomposition and packing
Open Honest solutions fit optimization and self-imposed goals
Graph Nodes constrain connection communication topology and protocol

Use the palette as a balance lever. Too loose trivializes the bottleneck; too tight funnels the player toward one architecture.

Separate reasoning from labour. If the idea arrives quickly and the remaining work is repeated placement, retiming, or waiting, shrink or reframe the puzzle.

Do this. Change the bottleneck before changing the numbers. Difficulty should come from a new decision, not a larger quota or smaller board by default.

Seek contrasting solution families

A solution family groups builds that share an architecture. Families differ in decomposition, topology, scheduling, use of state, or metric priority; layouts within one family differ only in detail.

For each open puzzle, construct two plausible same-palette references from different families.

Two references show possibility, not prevalence. Their purpose here is to expose a contract that accidentally permits only one recipe.

Scores alone do not show diversity. Unrelated machines can share a score, and one architecture can span many scores.

Use metrics only when they reshape the puzzle

A metric belongs to the abstract puzzle when it changes which architectures are attractive. Speed, space, resources, and robustness are useful only when their costs do not all move together.

If one solution wins every metric, there is no trade-off. Change the cost model, the palette, or the contract until different priorities favour different structures.

A hard limit defines validity. A metric compares valid solutions. Turn a metric into a gate only when satisfying that bound is the puzzle itself.

Histograms, rankings, sharing, and optimization culture are outside this guide. The only question here is whether a cost changes the solution space.

A puzzle is structurally sound when its contract is explicit, its bottleneck is named, one build proves solvability, and an open puzzle admits a plausible contrasting family.
